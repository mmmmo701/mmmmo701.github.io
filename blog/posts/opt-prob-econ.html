An Optimization Problem from Economics

I was reading about Economics and this optimization problem is so classical and interesting.

← Back to blog

I was reading an Economics book today, and I encountered this problem where there is item \(A\) and \(B\) with their own prices for each unit, and each item has a diminishing marginal benefits. Our goal is to maximize the total marginal benefit with a given budget. If you took AP Economics, you might remember that you were taught that the greedy algorithm always works. I am curious about why the greedy algorithm works.

Problem Formulation

Let \(B\) be your total budget and \(n\) be the maximum amount of one item you can buy. Let \(p_A\) be the price of item \(A\) and \(p_B\) be the price of item \(B\). Let \(a_1,a_2,\cdots,a_n\) be the marginal benefit of each of item \(A\), and \(b_1,b_2,\cdots,b_n\) be the marginal benefit of each of item \(B\). It is guarenteed that \[ a_1 \geq a_2 \geq \cdots \geq a_n \geq 0,\quad b_1 \geq b_2 \geq \cdots \geq b_n \geq 0. \] Now, the problem is to find \(k\) and \(\ell\) such that \(kp_A + \ell p_B \leq B\) and \[ \sum_{i=1}^k a_i + \sum_{i=1}^\ell b_i \] is maximized.

The Greedy Algorithm

The greedy algorithm looks like this.

  1. \((k_0,\ell_0) \gets (0,0)\)
  2. for \(i\) in \(1\) to \(2n\):
    1. if \(\frac{a_{k_i + 1}}{p_A} > \frac{b_{\ell_i + 1}}{p_B}\) and \(k_i + 1 \leq n\)
      1. \((k_i,\ell_i) \gets (k_{i-1}+1, \ell_{i-1})\)
    2. else
      1. \((k_i,\ell_i) \gets (k_{i-1}, \ell_{i-1}+1)\)
  3. \(\iota \gets \max \left\{i \in [2n] \mid k_ip_A + \ell_i p_B \leq B \right\} \)
  4. return \((k_\iota, \ell_\iota)\)

Why Does it Work

Assume Unit Price

We first assume \(p_A = p_B = 1\). Then the problem becomes choosing \(k\) and \(\ell\) such that \(k + \ell = B\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^k a_i + \sum_{i=1}^\ell b_i\) is maximized.

Assume \(k^*,\ell^*\) is the result by running the greedy algorithm, then \(a_{k^* + 1} < a_{\ell^*}\) and \(a_{\ell^* + 1} < a_{k^*}\). AFSOC this is not the most optimal solution, then there exists a better solution \(\tilde{k},\tilde{\ell}\).

Case 1: If \(k^* = \tilde{k}\), then \(\ell^* = \tilde{\ell}\). Then they are the same solution. Contradicts.

Case 2: If \(k^* \neq \tilde{k}\), then let \(\tilde{k},\tilde{\ell}\) be an optimal solution that minimizes \(\left| k^* - \tilde{k}\right|\). WLOG, assume \(k^* < \tilde{k}\). Then \(\ell^* > \tilde{\ell}\), which means that \(\ell^* \geq \tilde{\ell} + 1\). We know that \(b_{\tilde{\ell} + 1}\) was not selected while \(\tilde{k}\) was. By assumptions and the algorithm, we get \[ b_{\tilde{\ell} + 1} \geq b_{\ell^*} \geq a_{k^* + 1} \geq a_{\tilde{k}}. \] Therefore, \(\tilde{k} - 1,\tilde{\ell} + 1\) is a better solution with a smaller \(\left| k^* - \tilde{k}\right|\). Contradicts.
